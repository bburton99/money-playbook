# The Money Playbook
### A financial guide for guys ages 20–25 — no get-rich-quick schemes, just the boring stuff that works

Everything here reflects the broad consensus of time-tested personal finance practice: the Bogleheads philosophy, the "Financial Order of Operations," Dave Ramsey's Baby Steps (where they overlap with mainstream advice), and CFP-level planning norms. Where they disagree on details, this guide takes the mainstream middle. Figures reflect **2026 IRS limits** (Roth IRA $7,500; 401(k) $24,500).

---

## Why your 20s are the cheat code

Compound growth rewards **time** more than talent or income. $250/month invested at an 8% average annual return:

| Start age | Total contributed by 65 | Value at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | ~$129,000 | **~$1.12M** |
| 32 | ~$99,000 | **~$0.48M** |

Waiting ten years costs roughly **$640,000** — for only $30K less contributed. Nothing else in this guide matters as much as starting now.

## The 8 moves, in order

Do these in sequence. Each step assumes the previous one is handled.

### 1. Run your numbers (budget)
- Track every dollar for one month — you can't fix what you don't measure.
- **Tithing comes off the top.** Treat your 10% tithe as the *first* line item — paid on payday before anything else. Budgeting it first (rather than from "what's left over") is what makes it sustainable, and it builds the same discipline that makes every other step on this list work.
- Then apply the **50/30/20 rule** to what remains: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% saving/debt payoff. As a student, the percentages will be lumpy; the habit is what matters.
- **Pay yourself first**: move savings out on payday, automatically, before you can spend it.

### 2. Stash a starter emergency fund ($1,000)
- Put $1,000 in a **high-yield savings account (HYSA)** at a *different* bank than your checking, so it's slightly annoying to raid.
- This is what keeps a blown tire or an ER copay from becoming 22% APR credit card debt.
- Once you're working full-time, grow it to **3–6 months of essential expenses**. This money is boring on purpose — it's insurance, not an investment.

### 3. Avoid debt. Borrow on purpose.
The strongest financial position is simply not owing anyone. Every consensus framework — Bogleheads, Ramsey, CFP guidance — treats consumer debt as a claim on your future income that compounds *against* you.

**Avoid it in the first place:**
- **Never carry a revolving balance.** Credit cards are a payment tool, not a loan — if you can't pay the statement in full, you can't afford the purchase.
- **Minimize loans on depreciating assets — and stop worrying about the car you drive.** A new car loses roughly 20% of its value in year one and ~60% by year five, so financing one means paying interest on something falling in value. At this stage of life, a car has exactly one job: getting you there. **Function over form** — buy reliable and unremarkable, keep the payment minimal or nonexistent, and let the flex wait until compounding is working for you. If you do finance, the common guardrail is the **20/3/8 rule** (20% down, loan ≤ 3 years, payment ≤ 8% of gross income).
- **Use student loans wisely — demand a return on investment.** Education debt is only "good debt" when the degree pays for itself. Best-practice rules: keep **total borrowing below your expected first-year salary**; exhaust **federal loans** (fixed rates, income-driven repayment, forgiveness protections) before touching private ones; and borrow for tuition, not lifestyle.

**If you're already carrying high-interest debt:**
- Anything above roughly **7–8% APR** (cards are typically 20%+) gets attacked aggressively — no investment reliably beats that guaranteed "return."
- Use the **avalanche method**: minimums on everything, all extra money at the highest APR first. (If you need psychological wins, the snowball — smallest balance first — is a fine second choice. The best method is the one you finish.)
- **Federal student loans** at low fixed rates: pay on schedule; don't panic-prepay them ahead of investing, and never refinance them into private loans without understanding what protections you lose.

### 4. Build credit like an adult
- Get **one no-annual-fee credit card**, put a couple of recurring bills on it, and **autopay the statement balance in full** every month.
- Keep utilization under ~30% of the limit (under 10% is better).
- Carrying a balance does **not** build credit — that's a myth that costs you interest. Payment history and account age do the work.
- Never co-sign, and never use Buy-Now-Pay-Later as a lifestyle.

### 5. Take the free money (employer match)
- If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute **at least enough to capture the full match**. A 50–100% instant return exists nowhere else in finance.
- Check the vesting schedule so you know what's actually yours.
- No job with a match yet? Move on to step 6 and revisit at your first full-time job.

### 6. Invest boring, automate it
- Open a **Roth IRA** (you can contribute up to **$7,500 in 2026** if you have earned income). Your 20s — likely your lowest-tax-bracket years — are the ideal time to pay tax now and never again.
- Buy **low-cost, broadly diversified index funds** (a total-market or target-date fund is a complete portfolio). Keep expense ratios under ~0.20%.
- **Automate** contributions on payday. Then don't look. *Time in the market beats timing the market* — decades of data back this, and virtually no professional stock-pickers beat the index long-term after fees.
- Once high-interest debt is gone and the emergency fund is full, aim to invest **15–25% of gross income** across 401(k) + Roth IRA. If you have a high-deductible health plan, an **HSA** is a triple-tax-advantaged bonus account.
- This automated core is untouchable — the itch to pick stocks gets its own lane in step 8, *after* the fundamentals are secure.

### 7. Protect the downside (insurance)
- **Health insurance**: never go without. Under 26, you can usually stay on a parent's plan.
- **Renters insurance**: ~$15/month covers your laptop, bike, and liability. Get it.
- **Disability insurance**: your ability to earn is your biggest asset; take employer long-term disability coverage when offered.
- **Life insurance**: only when someone *depends on your income* (spouse, kid, co-signed debt). Then buy **cheap 20–30 year level term** (~10× income). **Skip whole life / universal life** — the mainstream consensus is that mixing insurance with investing serves the salesperson, not you. Be especially wary of "financial advisors" who target young graduates with permanent-life pitches.

### 8. Earn your swings (the 5–10% rule)
Once moves 1–7 are locked in — funded emergency reserve, no high-interest debt, match captured, 15–25% automated into index funds, downside insured — the time-tested frameworks open a small lane for higher-risk conviction. This isn't a loophole in the "boring" philosophy; it's part of it. **John Bogle himself recommended capping "fun money" at 5% of your portfolio** — the most you can afford to lose without jeopardizing retirement — and the broader **core-and-satellite** practice runs the speculative sleeve up to 10% at most.

- **What qualifies:** individual stocks you've actually researched, a dedicated fund building toward a real estate / rental down payment, seed money for a side venture, or other higher-risk positions. Your risk tolerance at 22 is a genuine asset — this is where it's allowed to work.
- **The house rules:**
  - **Cap it at 5–10% of your portfolio** and hold the cap through winning streaks — when the satellite outgrows its lane, skim the gains back into the index-fund core (that's rebalancing, and it forces you to sell high).
  - **Separate account.** The sandbox never touches the retirement core, physically.
  - **Never borrowed money, never leverage, never margin.** The whole design assumes you can lose it all and shrug.
  - **Losses are tuition.** If the sleeve goes to zero, your plan doesn't move an inch — that's the test that it was sized right.
- **The order matters more than the amount.** Deployed *after* steps 1–7, this is education and upside. Deployed *instead of* them, it's just the "wealth killers" list with better branding.

## Traps to skip (the "anti-portfolio")

These are the wealth-killers marketed hardest at men your age:

- **Day-trading, meme stocks, and crypto "signals"** — the data is brutal: the overwhelming majority of day traders lose money. Fast money content is entertainment, not a plan.
- **Sports betting apps** — engineered like slot machines, now living in your pocket. The house edge is the whole business model.
- **Whole-life insurance pitches** — see step 7. "It's a savings vehicle!" It's a commission vehicle.
- **Car payments that flex your image** — a $700/month truck payment on a $45K salary is the single most common wealth-killer for young men. Buy reliable and used; drive it long after it's paid off.
- **Lifestyle inflation** — every raise, split it: some to life, most to investments. Wealth is the gap between earning and spending.
- **"Passive income" course-sellers** — anyone selling you the secret to wealth already found theirs: selling courses.

## Rules of thumb to keep

1. **Pay God and yourself first.** Tithing and savings come out on payday, automatically, before you see the money.
2. **Spend less than you earn — invest the difference.** That one sentence is 90% of personal finance.
3. **If it sounds too good to be true, it is.** Real wealth compounds slowly, then suddenly.
4. **Your biggest asset is your earning power.** Finishing your degree, certifications, and skills usually beat any investment return available to you right now.
5. **Never leave a match on the table, never carry a card balance, never skip health insurance.**
